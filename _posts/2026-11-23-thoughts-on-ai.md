It's an interesting time to study mathematics (and related areas). My history teacher, Jacob, told us in the beginning of the Covid pandemic that the ancient Chinese had a saying: "May you live in interesting times". Don't be fooled; it is not a complement. The times that are most interesting to look back on are often the most turbulent times. To live in the interesting times often meant living through great uncertainty. Rebellions and changing dynasties for our ancient Chinese friends, and for us: a pandemic and AI in Math.

I try to follow the online "zeitgeist" for AI and Math. To follow it seems like following a pendulem: one day mathematics are a thing of the past and the following day, there has never been a better day to do mathematics. One issue is that mathematics has become an arena for the frontier labs to showcase their newest champion's ability. From a "outsider" (as in: I can understand the problem, but the theory behind is unknown) view, seeing the Riemann-hypotheses proved on a casual Thursday can feel underwhelming for such an achievement. On the other hand, most progress through human history must also have been achieved on some boring weekday, so why should mathematics be different?

The mathematics capabilities of AI-models can feel difficult to estimate. They have "solved" (plus-minus using the work of concurrent human mathematicians work) two millennium problems as today. So they know a thing or two about mathematics surely. As a human, I am left with a question whether I / we have anything more to contribute to mathematics. Perhaps the problems that the mathematics community pointed out as difficult in the 2000's only seems difficult to the human mind, and is trivial for the AI-"mind". I read somewhere an analogy for this, so no credit to myselft. It went something like this: Just like deer will eat away all the lower hanging tree-branches leaving only branches hanging high, mathematicians have perhaps solved all the "low-hanging" problems. For a giraffe this is no issue: it will happily eat away at the high branches. AI is maybe a giraffe compared to us. This silly analogy naturally leads to some further questions: What is out of reach for an AI / giraffe? Are us humans even able to conceptualise these problems? 

We can perhaps conceptualise these problems, and may even have done so already. Some problems may require completely new frameworks to solve, and I haven't encountered evidence that AI can establish the necessary framework. Thus Navier-stockes and the Riemann hypothesis only appeared difficult but where easy to solve. It might even be wrong to transfer humans deem difficult to what an AI might struggle with. I read somewhere that the things easy to automate are what humans struggle with, but the most difficult automation tasks are what humans deem easy. Thus automating mathematics is "easy", but to fold a shirt is tremendously difficult to automate / make a AI-robot do.

While it is entertaining - and confusing - to watch AI-giants tackle these enormous problems: the day-to-day work on the ground, so to speak, is also confusing. I, along with almost all of my fellow students, use AI in some capability for our coursework. Sometimes, it might be to solve difficult homework and other times it is to explain theoretical concepts. Often, we are meet with what seems like a convincing argument that must be correct. How do we check this? Usually, we don't. We take the explanation for granted and move on to the next homework problem. 

Is this the correct workflow for a student in the AI-era (side-note: should we already move away from the Anthropocene era to the AI-thropocene era?)? I don't think so.
